L00|In twenty eleven, Research In Motion had the best year in its history. Almost twenty billion dollars in revenue.
L01|Profit: three point four billion dollars. Enough to make it one of the most profitable technology companies on earth.
L02|Four years after the iPhone launched, BlackBerry was still growing. It sold forty-three percent more devices than the year before.
L03|The company everyone says the iPhone killed was, at that exact moment, at its peak.
L04|Then, in two years, it lost nearly all of it. And the thing that sold it to the world is the thing that took it down.
L05|This is how the keyboard built BlackBerry, and then trapped it.
L06|Research In Motion started in nineteen eighty-four in Waterloo, Ontario, a long way from Silicon Valley.
L07|Mike Lazaridis and Douglas Fregin started it. Jim Balsillie came in later and ran it with Lazaridis. None of them set out to build a phone.
L08|In nineteen ninety-nine they shipped a two-way pager with a tiny keyboard on the front. That was the first BlackBerry.
L09|The pitch was simple. An email arrives on your phone the second it is sent. No asking. No checking. It just appears.
L10|They called it push email, and it was the first version of a notification on a phone.
L11|You could answer on a real keyboard, with your thumbs. Nobody else in corporate America was doing that.
L12|That one advantage walked straight into the office. If your company ran its email on BlackBerry, you carried a BlackBerry.
L13|By the late two thousands it was the default phone of business. Bosses, lawyers, bankers, presidents.
L14|It was secure enough that governments relied on it. The United States government became one of its biggest customers.
L15|People had a name for how much they used it. They called it the CrackBerry.
L16|That is not a company with a product problem. That is a company with a licence to print money.
L17|Then in two thousand seven, Apple showed the iPhone. One big sheet of glass. No keyboard at all.
L18|The obvious assumption is that BlackBerry was finished that day. The numbers say otherwise.
L19|For years BlackBerry outsold it. Apple's first three iPhones were not enough to beat RIM's carrier deals or its grip on corporate email.
L20|And RIM saw it coming. Mike Lazaridis opened an iPhone, and told his staff exactly what he was looking at.
L21|According to the Globe and Mail's investigation, he said: they've put a Mac in this thing.
L22|He understood it was a computer. He just judged that business would not want one, because it could not type.
L23|The whole BlackBerry ran on thirty-two megabytes of memory. The iPhone's operating system alone took seven hundred.
L24|A computer needs the whole front of the phone to be screen. A keyboard needs the front of the phone to be keyboard.
L25|RIM could not have both. Every phone that kept the keyboard gave up screen, and everything a screen was for.
L26|Web pages, maps, video, photographs. A phone with a keyboard through the middle was a worse place to look at anything.
L27|That mattered more every year, because the software people wanted was not on the phone. It was in the app store.
L28|Developers build for the biggest screen and the biggest audience. BlackBerry had neither.
L29|RIM tried. In two thousand eight it released the Storm, its first touchscreen BlackBerry. It sold, and reviewers hated it.
L30|In twenty ten it announced a tablet, the PlayBook. It shipped late, half finished, and it flopped.
L31|RIM took a provision of four hundred and eighty-five million dollars against PlayBook stock it could not sell.
L32|To build the PlayBook and the phones after it, RIM bought a company called QNX. It was buying an operating system it should have built years before.
L33|The rest of the world was going somewhere else. In January twenty ten, BlackBerry was the most used smartphone platform in America, at forty-three percent.
L34|Almost twenty-two million people. That is the most it would ever reach in the United States.
L35|Two years later that had collapsed to nine million in America. The users did not go quiet. They moved.
L36|Even so, the company was still growing everywhere else. In twenty twelve it had eighty million users worldwide.
L37|Its own chief executive said it out loud. We have roughly eighty million users today. Nokia doesn't have that.
L38|But being big is not the same as being wanted. The market was leaving while the number of users was still rising.
L39|In October twenty eleven the network failed. For days, email and messages were down across five continents.
L40|In early twenty twelve, the two men who ran the company stepped down, in one afternoon. Thorsten Heins took over.
L41|Heins put everything on one bet. A brand new operating system, BlackBerry 10, that would finally answer the iPhone.
L42|By then the judgement was already written. According to the Globe and Mail's investigation, BlackBerry 10 was two years too late.
L43|In September twenty thirteen, the company reported a loss of about a billion dollars. Revenue had fallen by almost half.
L44|It cut four thousand five hundred jobs. That was about forty percent of everyone who worked there.
L45|Then came a rescue that was not a rescue. Fairfax, its largest shareholder, offered nine dollars a share, about four point seven billion, to take it private.
L46|Six weeks later the deal was gone. Instead, Fairfax led a one billion dollar cash injection, and BlackBerry stayed independent.
L47|And the man behind Fairfax had quit the board that August. The buyer walked out of the room before he made his offer.
L48|In November twenty thirteen a new chief executive arrived. John Chen. His job was not to win the market back. It was to keep the company alive.
L49|Chen did the thing nobody at BlackBerry could bring themselves to do for ten years. He stopped defending the phone.
L50|The company would keep the part it was actually good at. Security, software, and the systems that run inside cars.
L51|The phones were handed to other companies. TCL made them. Then a startup called OnwardMobility was going to.
L52|OnwardMobility never shipped a single phone. It shut down in twenty twenty-two with nothing to show.
L53|The last hardware was made in August twenty twenty. In January twenty twenty-two the old service was switched off for good.
L54|BlackBerry is still a company. It just isn't a phone company. Software, security, in-car systems. About three thousand people.
L55|The phone that every business leader carried is now a logo on handsets that are no longer made.
L56|So here is the real reason BlackBerry fell. Not that it missed the shift. It saw the iPhone in two thousand seven.
L57|It is that the shift was against its best product. To follow the market it had to give up the keyboard, which was the whole company.
L58|And a company will defend the thing that made it, right up until that thing is the reason it cannot move.
L59|The keyboard was not a mistake. It was a decision so good that the company forgot it was a decision.
L60|Every company has one of those. Something that worked so well that nobody can say out loud what it costs.
L61|This is Paper Empire. Next time: the store that sold everything, and lost to the idea it invented. Sears.
