# Nokia: "The Platform Switch" (draft 1 for approval)

**Channel:** Paper Empire · **Voice:** Kokoro am_adam at 0.9× · **Target:** 8:00–9:00 (1,100–1,250 words)
**Title options:** 1. Nokia Was Not Beaten by the iPhone 2. Nokia’s Fatal Platform Switch 3. How Nokia Lost the Phone War
**Thumbnail:** black-and-white Nokia handset beside a red platform-switch stamp; text: PLATFORM SWITCH.

**The twist:** Everyone remembers the iPhone beating Nokia. The stranger story is that Nokia still owned the market, then deliberately abandoned its own smartphone platform while the replacement was not ready.

## COLD OPEN
> In two thousand seven, Nokia shipped four hundred thirty-seven million mobile devices and held thirty-eight percent of the world market. [S1]
- Giant black number 437 MILLION; newspaper market-share stamp.

> Six years later, the phone business was being sold to Microsoft for five point four four billion euros. [S7]
- Red sale stamp over a Nokia handset photo.

> The popular explanation is simple: the iPhone arrived, and Nokia missed it. The real explanation is a chain of choices made while Nokia was still winning.
- iPhone silhouette enters beside Nokia crown, then a ruled line splits them.

> This is how the world’s biggest phone company turned a lead into a liquidation sale.
- Title card: NOKIA / THE PLATFORM SWITCH.

## CHAPTER 1: THE EMPIRE
> Nokia was not a niche brand waiting to be discovered. It had held the global device lead since nineteen ninety-eight. [S1]
- Timeline rule from 1998 to 2007.

> In two thousand seven, its mobile-device sales reached fifty-one point zero five eight billion euros, with seven point nine eight five billion euros in operating profit. [S2]
- Ledger page with the two figures, source line.

> It sold phones at every price: basic handsets, business devices, music phones, cameras, maps, and the Nseries smartphones.
- Black-and-white product spread, labels land on each phone.

> That year, Nokia shipped sixty point five million converged devices: phones designed to replace several separate gadgets. [S9]
- Count-up to 60.5 MILLION over a grid of phones.

> Nokia’s advantage was scale. A carrier could buy one brand for almost every customer, in almost every country.
- World map built from ruled lines, Nokia logo at center.

> The company had hardware factories, distribution, patents, and a software platform called Symbian. For years, that combination looked unbeatable.
- Symbian name printed as a foundation under a phone.

> But the market was about to stop rewarding the best phone catalogue and start rewarding the best software ecosystem.
- Catalogue sheet tears into an app-grid diagram.

## CHAPTER 2: THE NEW SCOREBOARD
> Apple announced the iPhone in January two thousand seven, combining a touch screen, a web browser, music, and a new way to install software. [S3]
- Apple announcement clipping; four feature words print in sequence.

> Nokia did not instantly disappear. It kept shipping millions of phones and remained the volume leader.
- Nokia shipment line continues across frame.

> The problem was that Symbian had been built around buttons, menus, and many hardware variations. The iPhone made the operating system the product.
- Split frame: keypad menus versus touch interface.

> Android then gave other manufacturers a fast, modern platform without requiring them to build one from scratch.
- Android wordmark appears as competitors’ phone outlines multiply.

> Nokia had experiments of its own, including Maemo and the later MeeGo. But experiments inside a giant company are not the same as a unified product strategy.
- Several platform labels overlap, then a grey rule separates them.

> The company’s scale became a complication. Different teams protected different products, regions, and road maps.
- Organizational chart fills the frame; lines tangle.

> While the market moved toward ecosystems, Nokia was still measuring success through devices shipped.
- Shipment counter runs; an app icon sits ignored at the edge.

## CHAPTER 3: THE SWITCH
> In February two thousand eleven, new chief executive Stephen Elop announced a broad partnership with Microsoft. Windows Phone would become Nokia’s primary smartphone platform. [S4]
- Dated strategy release, Microsoft and Nokia names underlined.

> Nokia’s own filing called twenty-eleven and twenty-twelve transition years and warned that the platform change could bring market-share losses. [S5]
- Red warning underline on “transition years.”

> The plan was rational on paper. Microsoft had software and developer tools. Nokia had hardware, maps, distribution, and a global brand.
- Two-column asset list joins with a ruled arrow.

> Nokia also said it had about two hundred million Symbian owners to transition over time. [S10]
- 200 MILLION printed over a crowd of handset silhouettes.

> But a transition creates a gap. Customers hesitate when the old platform is being phased out and the new one has not proved itself.
- Old platform stamp fades to grey; new platform remains blank.

> Nokia’s own report said Symbian sales were declining every quarter in two thousand twelve, and that it did not expect significant Symbian volumes in two thousand thirteen. [S6]
- Quarterly bars fall in black, final bar turns red.

> The company had announced the future before the future was ready. That announcement changed what customers, developers, and carriers expected immediately.
- Press headline spreads across a phone; app icons peel away.

## CHAPTER 4: THE FALL
> Nokia launched Lumia phones on Windows Phone, including the Lumia nine-twenty and eight-twenty in late two thousand twelve. [S6]
- Lumia product photo with model numbers sourced below.

> Reviews could be positive, but a platform needs more than a good handset. It needs apps, updates, carriers, and reasons to switch.
- Four ruled columns: apps, updates, carriers, users.

> Nokia’s mobile-device share fell from twenty-seven percent in two thousand eleven to twenty-one percent in two thousand twelve. [S6]
- Market-share bars drop; red mark appears once.

> In the annual-report series, the share reached fourteen percent in two thousand thirteen. [S8]
- 14% stamped in red beside the shrinking bar.

> The fall was not only smartphones. Cheaper Android phones improved quickly, squeezing Nokia from above and below.
- Two black arrows close around a Nokia handset.

> In a growing market, losing relevance is expensive because every delayed product also delays the ecosystem around it.
- Calendar pages fall behind a phone launch sheet.

> Nokia was still a large company. That made the decline look slower from inside than it felt to customers outside.
- Wide company silhouette contracts while a customer queue exits frame.

## CHAPTER 5: THE SALE
> On September third, two thousand thirteen, Microsoft agreed to buy substantially all of Nokia’s Devices and Services business for three point seven nine billion euros. [S7]
- Contract page; purchase price in black.

> Microsoft would also pay one point six five billion euros to license Nokia’s patents, for a total of five point four four billion euros. [S7]
- Two figures add on a ruled ledger; total receives red sale stamp.

> Nokia kept its network business, patents, and mapping operations. The handset empire that made the name famous was leaving the company.
- Nokia wordmark splits: phones slide away, networks remain.

> The deal closed in two thousand fourteen. Microsoft renamed the handset operation Microsoft Mobile, but the platform problem remained.
- New nameplate lands over an old Nokia phone.

> The lesson is not that hardware stopped mattering. Nokia proved hardware could still win attention. The lesson is that a platform switch can destroy the value of hardware before the replacement ecosystem exists.
- Phone shell remains; empty app grid behind it.

## CHAPTER 5.5: WHAT THE NUMBERS HID
> Nokia’s global volume could still look impressive even as the high-value smartphone business weakened. The totals mixed very different kinds of phones. [S6]
- One tall shipment bar splits into smartphones and basic phones.

> A basic handset sale kept the volume graph alive, but it did not create the same developer network, data usage, or upgrade cycle as a modern smartphone.
- Volume bar stays black; ecosystem bar turns grey.

> Nokia’s filings described a business in transition, with restructuring and falling Symbian volumes happening at the same time as the Windows Phone build-out. [S6]
- Filing pages stack beside a shrinking product line.

> That meant engineers were supporting an old system, building a new system, and trying to keep carriers supplied across dozens of markets.
- Three workstreams pull a single ruled line in different directions.

> Every extra month mattered because customers were learning new habits: touch navigation, app stores, cloud accounts, and constant software updates.
- Four habits print around a touch-screen phone.

> Nokia had resources, but resources do not automatically create speed. Large companies can spend more and still decide later.
- Heavy ledger lands on a stopwatch.

> The iPhone did not need to beat every Nokia model. It needed to change what a premium phone was judged against.
- Premium phone checklist changes from camera and battery to software and apps.

> Once that standard moved, Nokia’s strongest old advantages became less decisive. Distribution could put a phone in a store; it could not fill an app store.
- Storefront photo fades into an empty app grid.

> Microsoft brought a credible software company, but the partnership also tied Nokia’s fate to a platform competing against Apple and Android from behind.
- Three platform columns; Windows starts several steps back.

> Nokia’s strategy was a bet on coordination: two large companies could combine their strengths faster than the market changed.
- Partnership contract balanced on a scale.

> The market gave them far less time than the original plan had actually required originally.
- Calendar flips rapidly to 2013; red line cuts across it.

> The company could still point to millions of units, but the strategic clock was measured in developer commitments and customer upgrades. [S5][S6]
- Two clocks: shipment counter and ecosystem counter.

> That is why the collapse felt sudden in public, even though the warning signs were printed in Nokia’s own reports. [S5][S6]
- Annual reports stack into a falling timeline.
