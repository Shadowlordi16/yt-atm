L00|In two thousand seven, one company made almost four out of every ten mobile phones sold on Earth.
L01|It wasn't Apple. It wasn't Samsung. It was Nokia, a company from Finland.
L02|Six years later, Nokia sold its entire phone business to Microsoft.
L03|The story everyone tells is that the iPhone killed it. Nokia was too slow, too proud, and too late.
L04|But in two thousand eleven, four years after the iPhone, Nokia was still the biggest phone maker in the world.
L05|Then, in a single morning, it announced that its own phone software was finished, before the replacement even existed.
L06|This is how the world's biggest phone company burned its own platform. And how a paper mill survived it.
L07|The story starts in eighteen sixty-five, with a mining engineer named Fredrik Idestam.
L08|He built a wood pulp mill on the rapids in Tampere, in southern Finland.
L09|A few years later, he opened a second mill, beside a small town called Nokia. The town gave the company its name.
L10|For most of the next century, Nokia had nothing to do with phones. It made paper. Then rubber boots, car tires, and electrical cables.
L11|In nineteen sixty-seven, three Finnish companies merged into one Nokia Corporation: the paper company, the rubber works, and the cable works.
L12|The cable business led it into electronics, and electronics led it into telephones.
L13|On July first, nineteen ninety-one, Finland's prime minister made the world's first call on a GSM network, the digital standard most of the world's phones would soon use. Nokia helped build that network.
L14|Then, in nineteen ninety-two, a new chief executive, Jorma Ollila, made the biggest bet in Nokia's history.
L15|He sold almost everything that wasn't telecommunications. Paper, rubber, cables, televisions. All of it.
L16|Nokia would be a phone company. Nothing else.
L17|The bet paid off faster than anyone expected. In nineteen ninety-eight, Nokia passed Motorola to become the biggest mobile phone maker in the world.
L18|It would hold that title for fourteen years.
L19|Nokia phones were simple, tough, and cheap enough for almost anyone.
L20|In nineteen ninety-seven, the Nokia sixty-one ten came with a little game called Snake. For millions of people, it was the first game they ever played on a phone.
L21|The Nokia thirty-three ten sold around a hundred and twenty-six million units.
L22|The Nokia eleven hundred, a basic phone built for emerging markets, sold more than two hundred and fifty million. Guinness lists it as the best-selling mobile phone ever.
L23|In Lagos, in Delhi, in Jakarta, for millions of people, their first phone was a Nokia.
L24|And Nokia was already selling smartphones, years before most people used the word. In two thousand seven, it shipped about sixty million of what it called converged devices, phones that could also take photos, play music and browse the web.
L25|They ran on Nokia's own software, called Symbian.
L26|That year, Nokia shipped four hundred and thirty-seven million devices. Across the whole company, sales were about fifty-one billion euros.
L27|In January two thousand seven, Apple introduced the iPhone.
L28|It had no keyboard. It had one button, and a screen you touched with your finger.
L29|Less than two years later, the first phone running Google's Android went on sale.
L30|Suddenly, a phone wasn't just hardware. It was software, apps, and the developers who made them.
L31|Nokia knew how to build phones better than anyone. But Symbian had been designed for an older kind of phone, and making it work like an iPhone proved slow and hard.
L32|Nokia was still selling enormous numbers of phones. But in the most profitable part of the market, the high-end smartphone, the ground was moving.
L33|In September twenty ten, Nokia hired a new chief executive from outside Finland for the first time: Stephen Elop, who had run Microsoft's office software business.
L34|Five months later, on February eighth, twenty eleven, an internal memo from Elop to Nokia's staff leaked to the press.
L35|In it, he compared Nokia to a man standing on a burning oil platform in the North Sea. Stay, and burn. Or jump into the freezing water.
L36|His message was clear: Nokia's platform was on fire.
L37|The memo didn't hold back. "The first iPhone shipped in two thousand seven," Elop wrote, "and we still don't have a product that is close to their experience."
L38|He wrote that Android had just taken the lead in smartphone volumes. Not one company, but the whole Android platform, built by many phone makers at once.
L39|At the cheap end, he warned about Chinese manufacturers: "They are fast, they are cheap, and they are challenging us."
L40|And he named the real fight. "The battle of devices," he wrote, "has now become a war of ecosystems."
L41|Then the line that made headlines around the world: "We poured gasoline on our own burning platform."
L42|Three days later, Nokia made its jump.
L43|It announced a partnership with Microsoft. Windows Phone would become Nokia's main smartphone software.
L44|And Symbian, the software running on hundreds of millions of Nokia phones, would be phased out.
L45|Nokia itself counted around two hundred million people using Symbian phones. Every one of them had just been told their phone's software had no future.
L46|That day, Nokia's shares fell by more than ten percent.
L47|There was a problem. The first Nokia phones running Windows were still months away.
L48|So Nokia was telling its customers that the phones on the shelf had no future, while it had nothing new to sell them.
L49|Business writers have a name for this: the Osborne effect. Announce your next product too early, and people stop buying the current one.
L50|In the second quarter of twenty eleven, Nokia's smartphone sales were down by about a third from a year earlier.
L51|That same quarter, Apple and Samsung both shipped more smartphones than Nokia, for the first time.
L52|At the end of two thousand eleven, the first Nokia Windows phones, called Lumia, finally arrived.
L53|Reviewers liked the design. But the apps people wanted were on iPhone and Android, and buyers had already moved on.
L54|In the first quarter of twenty twelve, Samsung overtook Nokia as the world's biggest phone maker, ending Nokia's fourteen-year run at the top.
L55|By two thousand thirteen, Nokia's share of the phone market had fallen from thirty-eight percent to about fourteen.
L56|On September third, twenty thirteen, Microsoft agreed to buy Nokia's phone business and license its patents, for five point four four billion euros.
L57|Stephen Elop, the man who wrote the burning platform memo, went with the phones back to Microsoft.
L58|It didn't work for Microsoft either. In July twenty fifteen, it wrote off about seven point six billion dollars on the phone business, and announced up to seven thousand eight hundred job cuts.
L59|Here's the part most people miss. Nokia didn't die.
L60|It kept the business it had built alongside the phones: the networks that phones run on.
L61|In two thousand sixteen, it bought its French rival Alcatel-Lucent in a deal worth about fifteen point six billion euros.
L62|Today, Nokia builds the mobile networks that carry calls and data for operators around the world.
L63|A paper mill became a rubber company. A rubber company became a phone giant. And a phone giant became a network company.
L64|Reinventing itself is the one thing Nokia has always been good at.
L65|The iPhone didn't kill Nokia's phones on its own. Nokia was still on top four years later.
L66|What finished them was a decision: to declare its own platform dead, before the next one was ready.
L67|The lesson isn't "don't change." Nokia had to change. The lesson is about timing.
L68|Elop's memo was right that the platform was burning. But the announcement, made before the next phone was ready, may have poured on the gasoline he had warned about.
L69|Before you burn the platform you're standing on, make sure the next one can hold your weight.
