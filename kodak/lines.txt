L00|In April twenty twelve, Facebook paid one billion dollars for an app that took photos.
L01|The company had thirteen employees.
L02|Three months earlier, a company with a hundred and thirty years of photography behind it had filed for bankruptcy.
L03|That company was Kodak. And here's the part that makes it hurt: Kodak invented the digital camera.
L04|It built it in nineteen seventy-five. It studied the threat in nineteen eighty-one. It even became the number one digital camera seller in America.
L05|It still lost.
L06|This is the story of a company that saw the future and couldn't afford it.
L07|In eighteen eighty-eight, a bank clerk turned inventor named George Eastman sold a camera for twenty-five dollars. It came loaded with film for a hundred pictures.
L08|When the roll was full, you mailed the whole camera back to Rochester. Kodak developed the pictures, reloaded the camera, and sent it home.
L09|The slogan was four words long, and it changed the world. You press the button. We do the rest.
L10|In nineteen hundred, Kodak made it even cheaper. The Brownie camera cost one dollar.
L11|Kodak sold the idea as much as the camera. By nineteen fourteen, the smiling Kodak Girl was selling cameras from the pages of the Saturday Evening Post.
L12|And that was the trick. The camera was cheap. The money came after: the film, the chemicals, the paper, the processing. Every photo anyone took paid Kodak again.
L13|By nineteen seventy-six, Kodak sold ninety percent of the film and eighty-five percent of the cameras in the United States.
L14|At its peak, in nineteen eighty-eight, Kodak employed about a hundred and forty-five thousand people.
L15|And in nineteen ninety-six, its revenue was about sixteen billion dollars.
L16|Even in two thousand, digital made up only about twenty-one percent of Kodak's sales.
L17|But the most important thing Kodak ever built never went on sale.
L18|In nineteen seventy-five, a young Kodak engineer named Steve Sasson put together a strange machine. It was about the size of a toaster, and it weighed eight pounds.
L19|Its pictures were a hundred pixels by a hundred pixels. Saving one to a cassette tape took twenty-three seconds.
L20|It was the first digital camera.
L21|It took its first picture in December nineteen seventy-five.
L22|Sasson wasn't allowed to talk about it in public, or show the prototype to anyone outside the company.
L23|But the popular version, that Kodak locked it in a drawer, is too simple. Sasson spent the rest of his thirty-five years at Kodak working on digital imaging.
L24|It wasn't that Kodak didn't understand. In nineteen eighty-one, its head of market intelligence, Vince Barabba, led a major study comparing film with digital.
L25|According to later accounts, the finding was blunt. Digital photography had the potential to replace Kodak's film business.
L26|Kodak knew. The problem was what knowing meant.
L27|Every digital photo was a photo nobody had to print.
L28|Kodak kept building anyway. In March nineteen ninety-five, it released the DC40, one of the first digital cameras made for ordinary consumers.
L29|So Kodak tried to have it both ways. In two thousand one, it launched EasyShare, a family of digital cameras, printers and software, built to make digital feel simple.
L30|And it worked. By two thousand four, Kodak was the number one digital camera brand in America, with about twenty-two percent of the market, ahead of Sony for the first time.
L31|In two thousand five it held the top spot again, shipping about seven million cameras to American stores.
L32|But here's the trap. A camera sells once.
L33|Film sold every single time you took a picture. Kodak was winning a race where the prize was much smaller.
L34|And the old business was disappearing faster than the new one could replace it. In twenty oh-nine, Kodak announced the end of Kodachrome, its most famous film, after seventy-four years.
L35|Kodachrome was so famous that Paul Simon wrote a hit song about it, in nineteen seventy-three.
L36|Then the camera itself started to disappear.
L37|It moved into the phone. And the photos moved onto the internet, where nobody needed film, paper, or a Kodak.
L38|Kodak's inventions didn't disappear with it. Around twenty ten, phone makers Samsung and LG agreed to pay Kodak close to a billion dollars to license its digital imaging patents.
L39|The digital camera Kodak invented was now making money for Kodak inside other companies' phones.
L40|In twenty eleven, Kodak went after Apple too. It said its patent fight with Apple alone could be worth more than a billion dollars in royalties.
L41|On January nineteenth, twenty twelve, Eastman Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.
L42|By then its workforce was down to about nineteen thousand people, from a hundred and forty-five thousand at the peak.
L43|And it was carrying the weight of its own history. When it filed, Kodak had about twice as many retirees as workers.
L44|In Rochester, New York, the city Kodak built, the company that once employed tens of thousands now employed about five thousand.
L45|Weeks later, Kodak said it would stop making digital cameras altogether.
L46|That April, Facebook bought Instagram for one billion dollars, three hundred million in cash and about twenty-three million Facebook shares.
L47|To pay its creditors, Kodak sold off its digital imaging patents. About eleven hundred of them. Kodak had once said they could be worth up to two point six billion dollars.
L48|They sold for about five hundred twenty-five million.
L49|The buyers were groups that included Apple and Google, the companies that had put a camera in every pocket.
L50|The same Apple Kodak had said owed it more than a billion.
L51|In September twenty thirteen, Kodak came out of bankruptcy, as a much smaller company focused on business customers.
L52|Kodak's great rival faced the exact same problem. Fujifilm.
L53|Fujifilm squeezed as much money as it could out of film, prepared for digital, and went looking for whole new businesses.
L54|In two thousand seven, it even launched a skincare line, Astalift, using the antioxidant chemistry it had developed for photographic film.
L55|Today, Fujifilm describes itself as a healthcare, materials and imaging company.
L56|Kodak was not blind to the future. It saw it clearly, more than once. What it couldn't do was act on what it saw.
L57|Kodak invented the future. It just couldn't make the future pay like the past.
L58|And that's the hardest kind of disruption to survive. Not the one you don't see coming. The one you see, and can't afford.
